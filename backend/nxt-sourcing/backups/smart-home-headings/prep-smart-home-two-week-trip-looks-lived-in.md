There's a particular kind of holiday anxiety that hits somewhere around the second glass of airport wine: did I leave the hall light on the whole time? Is the porch stacked with parcels? Does the house look, from the pavement, exactly like a house whose owners are 1,400 miles away?

The good news is that a modern smart home is very good at pretending. Not the clumsy 1990s version — one lamp on a mechanical timer clicking on at 7pm sharp for fourteen nights running — but a layered, slightly random performance that reads as ordinary life. It takes about an hour to set up properly, and it's the single best hour you can spend before a long trip.

## Start With a Lighting Script, Not a Timer

The giveaway of an empty house isn't darkness. It's *predictability*. A single lamp switching on at the same minute every evening tells anyone paying attention that nobody is home. Real households are messy: the kitchen goes on before the living room, someone leaves a landing light burning, the bedroom lamp dies at a different time on a Friday than on a Tuesday.

So build a script across three or four rooms rather than one. Aim for something like: kitchen from dusk to around 21:30, living room 19:00 to 23:00, a landing or hallway light for a short burst late, and a bedroom lamp that fades out somewhere between 22:40 and 23:50. Most apps — Hue, Google Home, Alexa Routines, SmartThings — let you add a randomised offset of ±15 or ±30 minutes. Turn that on for every single automation. It's the difference between a schedule and a life.

Use sunset-based triggers rather than clock times, too. If you're away for a fortnight, sunset can shift by 20 minutes, and lights coming on in broad daylight is its own kind of billboard.

Colour-capable bulbs help more than you'd expect, because you can warm the living room down to a lamplight tone in the evening rather than blasting cool white. If you're buying bulbs specifically for this, get ones that hold their schedule locally so a brief Wi-Fi wobble doesn't blank out your whole performance.

One more trick: leave one upstairs light on a very short cycle — four or five minutes, twice an evening, at random. Nothing says "someone just went to fetch something" like a bathroom light that behaves like a human bladder.

## Fake the Small Signals: Plugs, Sound and Motion

Lights are the headline, but the supporting cast matters. A smart plug is the cheapest way to animate things that aren't bulbs — a floor lamp, a radio, a TV backlight strip, the fish tank light. Put a plug on a small speaker or an old radio and run it for 40 minutes in the early evening. Muffled talk radio through a closed window is remarkably convincing, and far less odd than a TV blaring at 3am.

Energy-monitoring plugs earn their keep on longer trips for a second reason: you can glance at the app from the beach and see whether the fridge is still drawing power. A silent fridge for 12 days is a homecoming you don't want.

If you have a smart speaker or display, consider a mid-morning routine that plays a couple of minutes of music at low volume. And check the reverse: mute or disable any announcement that broadcasts "Front door unlocked" to an empty house, and turn off drop-in features you won't be using. Also worth a thought — motion-activated indoor lighting should be *disabled* while you're away, or your cat will file a very detailed report on your absence patterns.

Don't overdo it. Five well-chosen signals beat twenty. A house lit up like a stadium at 2am looks stranger than a dark one.

## Let the Front Door Do the Talking

The doorstep is where an empty home is usually discovered: a wedged flyer, an untouched parcel, three days of post visible through the glass. Pause deliveries in your Amazon and grocery accounts, ask a neighbour to clear the mat, and — if your postal service offers a hold-mail option — use it.

Then give yourself eyes. A video doorbell is the single most useful travel gadget you'll never pack, because it lets you answer the door from a hotel room. Answering matters more than watching: a courier or an opportunist hearing a live voice say "leave it round the side, thanks" gets an unambiguous message that the house is monitored. Set up a package-detection alert if your model supports it, and if you're leaving a battery model, charge it to 100% the day before — cold weather and heavy motion traffic drain batteries faster than the spec sheet suggests.

While you're there, tune your motion zones down. Two weeks of alerts from a busy pavement will train you to ignore your phone by day three, which defeats the point entirely. Narrow the zone to your path and door, raise the sensitivity threshold, and consider person-only alerts.

## Do the Boring Infrastructure Jobs the Day Before

Every clever automation in this article depends on two things: power and internet. Both deserve five minutes of attention.

- **Reboot the router** and, if it supports it, schedule a weekly automatic restart. A hung router on day three means a dead house for eleven days.
- **Check your hub's backup**. If everything runs through one bridge or hub, put it on an uninterruptible power supply if you have one, or at minimum on a socket nobody will unplug.
- **Give a trusted neighbour access** — a keypad code with a limited schedule, or a guest code you delete on return. Tell them what's automated so they don't panic-call you about lights.
- **Update firmware now, not later.** You do not want a device rebooting into a failed update while you're in another time zone.
- **Turn off what should be off**: water heater to holiday mode, heating to a frost-protection setpoint, iron and coffee machine unplugged, washing machine tap closed.
- **Save offline copies** of your alarm code, insurer's number and a neighbour's contact — somewhere that doesn't require the phone you might lose.

## Test the Whole Thing for 48 Hours

This is the step almost everyone skips. Two days before you fly, run your holiday scene as if you'd already gone, and then watch it. Walk outside after dark and look at your own house from the pavement. Is anything obviously robotic? Is a curtain-facing lamp casting a silhouette of an empty room? Is the porch light on all night when it never normally is?

Set one recurring reminder for mid-trip — day seven, say — to open the app, glance at camera snapshots, confirm the plugs still show power draw, and check nothing has dropped offline. Two minutes over breakfast, and then you can genuinely stop thinking about it.

Because that's the real goal here. Not a fortress, not a Hollywood decoy. Just a home that quietly gets on with looking normal while you get on with the holiday you actually paid for.
