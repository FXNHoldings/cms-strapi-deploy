There's a particular kind of pre-holiday stress that has nothing to do with packing. It's the key handover: the awkward coffee with your sitter three days early, the spare set you can't find, the neighbour who is now responsible for a set of brass keys with your address on the fob. It's 2024's problem solved with 1974's technology.

A smart lock with keypad entry turns that entire ritual into a two-minute job you can do from an airport lounge. You create a code, you send it, and when you land back home you delete it. No copies floating around, no lockbox bolted to your drainpipe advertising that you're away, and — this is the underrated part — a timestamped log that tells you your sitter actually turned up to feed the cat on Tuesday.

## Why a keypad beats a spare key for house sitters

A physical key has one setting: permanent. Once it leaves your hand you have no idea how many copies exist, and taking access away means changing the barrel. A keypad code is the opposite — it's disposable by design.

The practical wins stack up quickly. You can create a code that only works for the fortnight you're away, so it dies quietly whether or not you remember to revoke it. You can set time windows, which is handy if a cleaner comes Thursday mornings and doesn't need 24-hour access. You get an entry log, so if the plant-watering was supposed to happen every other day you can see whether it did. And crucially, your sitter doesn't need to carry anything. No keys to lose on a night out, no panicked WhatsApp at midnight.

There's also a subtler benefit for anyone letting a stranger into their home via a sitting platform: you never hand over anything that identifies your address. A four-to-eight digit code is useless the moment you change it.

## Keypad, app or fob — which access method to give your sitter

Most modern smart locks offer three or four ways in, and the right answer for a house sitter is usually not the one you use yourself.

**Keypad codes** are the workhorse. They're device-agnostic, they work if your sitter's phone dies, and they don't require them to download anything or create an account. For a two-week sit, this is almost always the right default.

**App access** — inviting your sitter as a guest user in the lock's app — gives you finer control and lets them unlock remotely, but it's a bigger ask. They have to install software, sign up, and trust you with their email. Reserve it for sitters you use repeatedly.

**Wallet or phone keys** (Apple Home Key, Google Home's digital equivalents) are wonderfully slick if your sitter happens to be on the same ecosystem, and dreadful if they aren't. Don't build your handover around them.

**Fobs and cards** are worth mentioning for one reason: they're a great backup to leave *inside* a keysafe as a fallback, rather than the primary method.

My strong advice: pick one primary method, add one backup, and explain both in writing. Three methods explained badly is worse than one explained well.

## Setting everything up from your phone before you fly

Here's the bit people underestimate. Your smart lock is only as reliable as the phone you administer it from, because the moment you're abroad, that phone *is* your front door.

Before you leave, check three things. First, that your lock's app is signed in and working over mobile data, not just your home Wi-Fi — test it by turning Wi-Fi off and locking the door from the driveway. Second, that push notifications are enabled, so an unlock at 3am actually reaches you. Third, that you have a roaming plan or eSIM sorted, because a lock app you can't reach is just an expensive doorknob.

If you're on iOS and use Apple Home, keeping your lock inside the Home app means you can check its status from the Lock Screen, share access via a Home invitation, and get notifications that survive being buried in an inbox.

Whichever side you're on, do the setup at least a week before departure. Smart lock apps have a habit of demanding a firmware update at the worst possible moment, and you don't want that happening in a taxi to the airport.

## Building a handover pack your sitter can actually use

Write it down. Everything. A verbal walkthrough at the door evaporates the second you leave, and your sitter will be too polite to message you on holiday about something they think they should remember.

A good handover note fits on one page and covers: the code and exactly how to enter it (does it need a # at the end? Does the keypad wake on touch?), what to do if the batteries die, where the mechanical key backup lives, the Wi-Fi password, bin days, alarm code and disarm sequence, and one emergency contact who lives locally and can physically get in.

That battery point deserves emphasis. Almost every smart lock lockout story starts with flat batteries. Replace them before a long trip regardless of the level shown, leave a spare set on the hall table, and make sure there is a physical key somewhere your sitter can reach — a keysafe out of sight at the side of the house is fine, provided the code differs from your door code.

## Handling problems from 6,000 miles away

Things will occasionally go sideways: the broadband drops, the lock loses its bridge, the code you set never synced. The fix is redundancy, not perfection.

One trick that costs almost nothing is leaving an old phone plugged in at home on Wi-Fi as a spare screen and hub — useful for running a camera feed, keeping a smart home app logged in, and giving your sitter a device to call you on if their own phone fails. A cheap, dependable handset with a big battery is ideal for this.

::product:samsung-galaxy-a35-128gb::

Beyond that, keep expectations sane. Agree a check-in message on day one so you know entry worked. Don't watch the door log obsessively — you're on holiday, and a sitter who feels surveilled won't sit for you again. If you have indoor cameras, tell them where the cameras are and switch off any in living spaces. That honesty is both the decent thing to do and, in many places, the legal one.

Do the boring preparation, and the handover becomes what it should be: a text message with six digits in it, sent from seat 14C.
