Long trips are wonderful right up until the moment, somewhere over the Atlantic, when you wonder whether you left the heating on. A month away is long enough that small mistakes compound: an always-on thermostat quietly burns through a winter's worth of gas, a slow drip becomes a ceiling stain, and a dozen idle gadgets sip power for four straight weeks.

The good news is that a smart home is genuinely brilliant at this job — if you spend an hour setting it up properly before you leave. Here's the pre-trip routine I run before every long journey.

## Set Your Heating Low, Not Off

The single biggest mistake travellers make in cold climates is switching the heating off entirely. It feels thrifty, and it's the fastest route to a burst pipe. Water expands as it freezes, and a pipe running through an uninsulated loft, garage or exterior wall cavity can split long before the rest of the house feels properly cold.

The safe number is a background temperature of around 12–15°C (55–60°F). Set that as a constant hold on your smart thermostat rather than a schedule — schedules assume someone is coming home at 6pm and will happily spike the temperature every evening for a month. Most thermostats (Nest, ecobee, Tado, Hive, Honeywell) have a dedicated "Away", "Eco" or "Holiday" mode that does exactly this and pauses learning so your usual routine isn't rewritten by four weeks of silence.

A few extras that cost nothing:

- Open interior doors and, if pipes run behind them, kitchen and bathroom cabinet doors, so warm air circulates to the coldest plumbing.
- Leave loft hatches slightly ajar if your cold water tank lives up there.
- If you have smart radiator valves, turn them **up** slightly in the rooms with vulnerable plumbing and down in interior rooms — that's where the real savings are.
- In genuinely severe climates, ask whether draining down the system or shutting off the stopcock makes more sense than heating an empty house at all.

If you're leaving in high summer, the mirror image applies: set cooling to a high hold (around 29°C/85°F) rather than off, so humidity doesn't run wild and warp floors or grow mould.

## Put a Sensor Where Water Would Actually Appear

Smart leak detectors are the cheapest insurance in the entire smart home category, and most people put them in the wrong place. A puck-style water sensor should sit on the floor **under** the washing machine, dishwasher, boiler or water heater, beside the toilet cistern, and under the kitchen sink — not in the middle of the room where a leak arrives last.

Pair them with cheap temperature sensors in the coldest corners of the house: the garage, the loft, the utility room on the north wall. Set an alert to fire at 5°C (40°F) so you get warning before anything freezes, not after. Most ecosystems let you automate a response — if the loft sensor drops below 5°C, bump the boiler up two degrees for an hour.

The gold-standard upgrade is an automatic shut-off valve on the main water supply (Moen Flo, LeakSmart, Grohe Sense Guard and similar). It closes the mains the moment it detects an abnormal flow, which is the difference between a damp patch and a four-week flood. If that's out of budget, the low-tech version is free: turn the stopcock off at the wall before you leave, and flush the toilets to drain the cisterns.

## Kill Phantom Load Without Killing the Fridge

Standby power is boring and it adds up. Over a month, a home office full of chargers, a TV, a soundbar, a games console and a couple of set-top boxes can quietly eat a meaningful chunk of a bill for a house nobody is living in.

Work through the house with a simple rule: if it doesn't preserve food, protect the property or run the network, it gets unplugged or scheduled off at the smart plug. Keep powered:

- The fridge and freezer
- The router, hub and anything your cameras or sensors depend on
- The boiler/furnace and its controls
- Security devices and one or two lights on a randomised schedule

Power down everything else. While you're at it, set your energy-monitoring plugs to log usage — coming home to a month of baseline data is the easiest way to find the one device that's been costing you money all year.

One more thing worth doing: switch security lights from motion-triggered floodlights to a believable, randomised indoor lighting routine. "Lived in" beats "lit up like a stadium" for deterrence, and it uses a fraction of the electricity.

## Keep Watch From the Road

Every alert you've just configured is worthless if it lands on a phone that's out of battery in a hotel safe. Before a long trip, check that your camera, thermostat and leak-sensor apps all have critical notifications enabled and that they can break through your phone's focus modes and Do Not Disturb.

Battery endurance matters more than megapixels here. A phone that comfortably lasts a day of airports and sightseeing is the one that will still be awake at 2am when a temperature sensor cries for help.

## Build a Plan That Doesn't Depend on You

Technology fails. Broadband drops, power cuts reset hubs, and cloud services have outages — and none of that helps if you're eight time zones away.

So finish with the human layer. Give one trusted neighbour, friend or family member a key, the stopcock location, your boiler's basic quirks and guest access to your camera or alarm system. Tell them what "normal" looks like so a false alarm doesn't send them over at midnight. Put your plumber's number and your insurer's emergency line in a note you can find on your phone offline.

Then do a final walk-through the morning you leave: heating on hold, stopcock decision made, sensors showing green in the app, non-essentials unplugged, windows locked, bins out, mail redirected or collected. Check your home insurance policy too — many have a clause requiring the heating to be maintained or the water drained if a property is unoccupied beyond 30 days, and a month-long trip sits right on that line.

Do all of that and the only thing waiting for you at the door is a pile of post.
